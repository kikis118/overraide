# OVERR<span class="ai">AI</span>DE

Copyright Kristians 08/10/2026

![Three frames from OVERRAIDE: a wall of screens, an eye with a monitor reflected in it, and an editor at a desk in the dark.](/media/proposal-frames.jpg)

Frames from OVERRAIDE (Kristians, shot 30th September – 4th October 2026)

OVERRAIDE is a 2.5-minute short about AI changing the world, and how it's changed the way I work. I've edited professionally for six years, a lot of it YouTube work that millions of people watch. My whole job is deciding what they see, and nobody sees me do it. This summer I started using an AI tool for parts of that job. I didn't realise how powerful it was until it had already changed how I work, and that took a few weeks. The film follows that, in one room with no dialogue: one person at a desk, and a job turning into something else while I'm still sitting in it. It's personal. I'm not going to tell you if it's good or bad, that's for whoever's watching.

Above are three frames from the film. A wall of screens running an automated process, an eye with a monitor reflected in it, and an editor alone in the dark.

I keep cutting back to the same eyes. That's a nod to Kuleshov, who cut the same face against different things and audiences read a different emotion each time (Prince and Hensley 1992). The eye never changes. What changes is what it's looking at, and what you bring to it. That's where I want the judgement to sit.

It's all shot in one room on a Sony FX30 with cine primes, lit by two monitors and two bike lights, nothing else. The screens carry real content, filmed directly, so the moiré, banding and reflections are real and happen in camera. Most of the film is in a really wide frame, around 3:1, like the frames above. It narrows what you can see and builds intensity.

The experimental part is Gaussian splatting. I reconstruct the empty room as a 3D scene so the camera can move in ways nobody could shoot. The whole film is an experiment in what new tools like this can do as a filmmaking medium, tools that are really cool and barely used in cinema. There's a history of that in experimental film: John Whitney made Catalog (Whitney 1961) on a repurposed military analog computer, and Michael Snow's La Région Centrale (Snow 1971) put the camera on a machine that moved in ways no operator could, which is the freedom the splat gives me.

For references I'm looking at Vertov's Man with a Movie Camera (Vertov 1929), where the editor is part of the film, and Farocki's Eye/Machine (Farocki 2001–03), images made by machines for machines. Arnulf Rainer (Kubelka 1960) and The Flicker (Conrad 1966) are there for the extreme of rhythm in the cut. I borrow the idea of the cut getting faster, just a lot less radically.
