Frames from OVERRAIDE (shot 1st October – 4th October 2026). These frames are work in progress: the film is in post-production right now.

OVERRAIDE is a 2-3 minute short about AI changing the world, and how it’s changed the way I work. I’ve edited professionally for six years, a lot of it is YouTube work. I’ve edited over 1000 videos with almost a hundred million views in total over those six years, and my whole job is deciding what those viewers see, but nobody sees me do it. This summer I started using an AI tool for parts of that job. I didn’t realise how powerful it was until it had already changed how I work, and that took a few weeks. The film follows that, in one room with no dialogue - one person at a desk, and a job turning into something else while I’m still sitting in it. It’s personal. I’m not going to tell you if it’s good or bad, that’s for whoever’s watching.

Above are three frames from the film. A wall of screens running an automated process, an eye with a monitor reflected in it, and a video editor alone in the dark.

In the short, I’ll keep cutting back to the same eyes. That’s a nod to Kuleshov, who cut the same face against different things so audiences read a different emotion each time. How well that original experiment really worked is debated (Prince and Hensley 1992), but later research did find the effect (Mobbs et al. 2006). The eyes never change, many of the shots will be reused on purpose throughout. What changes is what it’s looking at, and how it makes the viewer feel.

It’s all shot in one room on a Sony FX30 with cine primes, lit by two monitors and two bike lights, nothing else. It’s a one-person shoot, so everything you see was set up and filmed on my own. I’ve built tools specifically for the film that run and simulate how these AI tools work, in real time, and I film the screens while it happens. So none of it is CGI or composited screens - you will see the AI move the mouse live, cutting clips in the editing software and making progress as it goes. That’s also why the moiré, banding and reflections are real and happen in camera. Most of the film is in a really wide frame, around 3:1, like the frames above, so I can control exactly what the viewer needs to see and builds intensity.

The whole film is an experiment in what new tools can do as a filmmaking medium, tools that are really cool and barely used in cinema. One of them is Gaussian splatting: I’ll reconstruct the empty room as a 3D scene so the camera can move in ways nobody could shoot. There’s a history of that in experimental film: John Whitney made Catalog (Whitney 1961) on a repurposed military analog computer, and Michael Snow’s La Région Centrale (Snow 1971) put the camera on a machine that moved in ways no operator could, which is the freedom the splat gives me.

For references I’m looking at Vertov’s Man with a Movie Camera (Vertov 1929), where the editor is part of the film, and Farocki’s Eye/Machine (Farocki 2001–03), images made by machines for machines. Arnulf Rainer (Kubelka 1960) and The Flicker (Conrad 1966) are there for the extreme rhythm in the cut. I borrow the idea of the cut getting faster, just a lot less radically.
